# Mobile Game Scam Ads Research

## What Are These Games?

The screenshots show examples of **misleading mobile game advertisements** — a widespread practice in the mobile gaming industry where ads show exciting, simple gameplay that **does not exist in the actual game**.

## Games Identified From the Screenshots

| Game | What the Ad Shows | What the Game Actually Is |
|------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| **Last Z: Survival Shooter** | Tower defense shooter with armies of zombies | Generic base-building strategy game |
| **Last Fortress: Underground** | Zombie survival shooter action gameplay | Base-building/resource management strategy |
| **Last War: Survival Game** | Number merge puzzles, simple shooter minigames | Pay-to-win base-building strategy (Clash of Clans clone) |

All three are part of the same trend: **the advertised gameplay is completely different from the actual game**.

## Common Fake Ad Formats

### 1. Pull-the-Pin Puzzles
Originally created by Hero Wars developers for promotional purposes. Shows pin-pulling puzzle mechanics that don't exist in the actual game. The player in the ad always fails on purpose to make viewers want to "do it correctly."

### 2. Number Merge/Combine Games
Ads show simple number puzzles where you merge units to create bigger numbers to defeat enemies. Satisfying and easy to understand — but not the real game.

### 3. Tower Defense / Horde Shooter
Ads show a character shooting waves of enemies (like the zombie hordes in the screenshots). Designed to look action-packed and engaging.

### 4. "Save Them" / Rescue Scenarios
Drag-and-drop puzzle scenarios where you rescue characters from danger. Popularized by Playrix (Gardenscapes/Homescapes).

### 5. Intentionally Bad Gameplay
The person playing in the ad is **always terrible on purpose**. The psychology: create an ad so unsatisfying that viewers download the game thinking "I can do better than that."

## Why Do They Do This?

- **It works.** These misleading ads generate massive download numbers.
- **Low regulation.** The FTC and most regulators rarely enforce false advertising rules on mobile games.
- **Cheap production.** The minigame ads are simple to produce and A/B test at scale.
- **Playrix alone produced over 25,000 fake ads** in one year.
- The "pull the pin" format was so popular that by August 2020, Google Play had **250+ games** when searching "pull the pin" — many created to capitalize on demand generated by fake ads.

## Major Offenders

| Developer/Game | Known For |
|---------------|-----------|

| **Evony: The King's Return** | Pioneer of the misleading ad strategy |

| **Hustle Castle** | Pull-the-pin fake ads |

## The Actual Games Behind the Ads

Almost all of these games are actually one of:
- **Base-building strategy games** (like Clash of Clans)
- **Match-3 puzzle games** (like Candy Crush)
- **Idle/incremental games**

They are typically **free-to-play with aggressive pay-to-win microtransactions**.

## Has Anything Been Done About It?

- **UK (2020):** The Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) banned Playrix ads for Homescapes/Gardenscapes after complaints. Fine print saying "Not all images represent actual gameplay" was not enough.
- **YouTube/Game Theory:** The channel called out mobile game devs for potentially illegal advertising practices.
- **FTC (US):** Has rarely taken action against mobile game false advertising.
- **Overall:** Regulation remains minimal, and the practice continues largely unchecked.

## The Irony

The demand created by fake ads led to actual games being developed that match the ad gameplay:
- **Hero Rescue** — actually plays like the pull-the-pin ads
- **Save the Girl** — matches the rescue scenario ads
- Various "pull the pin" clones on app stores

The fake ads inadvertently created a real game genre.
